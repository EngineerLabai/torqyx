## The module defines the geometric scale of a gear

In metric mechanical design, the **module** (m) is the foundational dimension that establishes the physical scale of gear teeth. Mathematically, it is the ratio of pitch circle diameter (d) to the number of teeth (z):

**m = d / z**

where:
- **m** is the module in millimeters (mm)
- **d** is the reference pitch diameter in millimeters (mm)
- **z** is the integer number of teeth

From the module, all other basic cylindrical gear dimensions are derived:
- **Circular pitch (p)**: The arc distance along the reference circle between adjacent teeth: **p = π · m**
- **Addendum (h_a)**: Height of tooth above the pitch circle: **h_a = 1.0 · m** (for standard full-depth teeth)
- **Dedendum (h_f)**: Depth of tooth space below the pitch circle: **h_f = 1.25 · m**
- **Total tooth depth (h)**: **h = h_a + h_f = 2.25 · m**
- **Tip diameter (d_a)**: Outside diameter: **d_a = d + 2 · m = m · (z + 2)**
- **Root diameter (d_f)**: Bottom diameter: **d_f = d - 2.5 · m = m · (z - 2.5)**

For two gears to mesh smoothly together in conjugate motion, they must share the identical module and identical pressure angle (standardized at 20° under ISO 53).

## Raw geometric calculation vs. standardized module selection

When beginning a gearbox concept, dividing target pitch diameter by desired tooth count usually yields an arbitrary non-integer decimal (for example, m = 3.14 mm or m = 4.37 mm). However, production gears should never be manufactured with non-standard modules unless an aerospace mass-minimization constraint strictly mandates custom hobs and cutters.

Gear cutting tooling (hobs, shaper cutters, and grinding disks) is produced in standardized preferred metric series defined by **ISO 54** and **DIN 780**:

- **Series 1 (Primary preferred)**: 0.5, 0.6, 0.8, 1.0, 1.25, 1.5, 2.0, 2.5, 3.0, 4.0, 5.0, 6.0, 8.0, 10.0, 12.0, 16.0, 20.0 mm
- **Series 2 (Secondary choice)**: 1.125, 1.375, 1.75, 2.25, 2.75, 3.5, 4.5, 5.5, 7.0, 9.0, 11.0, 14.0, 18.0 mm

Standardizing on Series 1 drastically reduces manufacturing lead time, inspection tooling expenses, and spare part inventory costs across the equipment lifecycle.

## Core criteria for selecting an optimal module

Selecting a module is not a standalone geometric exercise; it involves balancing mechanical load capacity against acoustic noise, envelope size, and tooth kinematics:

1. **Tangential force and tooth bending fatigue**:
   Transmitted torque T produces a pitch-line tangential force:
   **F_t = (2 · T) / d**
   Larger modules create thicker tooth roots, resisting Lewis bending stress and cyclic fatigue under shock loads.

2. **Contact stress (pitting and surface durability)**:
   While root bending strength increases linearly with module, surface contact durability (Hertzian contact pressure) is primarily governed by pitch diameter, face width, and material surface hardness (case-carburizing, nitriding, or induction hardening). Increasing module without increasing pitch diameter does not solve surface pitting issues.

3. **Pinion tooth count and undercut risk**:
   For standard 20° involute gears without profile shift, cutting teeth with a standard rack generates **undercut** (material removal at the dedendum root) whenever the tooth count drops below 17:
   **z_min = 2 / sin²(α) = 2 / sin²(20°) ≈ 17.1**
   When designing a compact pinion with z < 17, engineers must apply a positive profile shift coefficient (x > 0) to avoid weakening the tooth root or reducing the active contact ratio.

4. **Pitch line velocity and acoustic noise**:
   At high rotational speeds (v > 10 m/s), smaller modules with larger tooth counts generate higher contact overlap ratios (contact ratio ε_α > 1.4), producing significantly lower vibration, smoother mesh transition, and quieter gearbox operation than coarse teeth.

## Sizing procedure and iterative refinement

A reliable design process follows these steps:

1. **Determine required gear ratio** and estimate initial pitch diameters based on available center distance.
2. **Compute raw module** m_raw = d / z and round to the nearest ISO 54 preferred standard module m_std.
3. **Recalculate exact reference diameters** d = m_std · z and center distance a = m_std · (z₁ + z₂) / 2.
4. **Verify root bending strength** according to ISO 6336-3 and contact safety according to ISO 6336-2.
5. **If bending safety is insufficient**, evaluate whether widening the face width b (typically b ≈ 8–12 · m for spur gears) or specifying higher grade alloy steel (such as 18CrNiMo7-6 or 42CrMo4) is preferable before unnecessarily upsizing to a larger module.
