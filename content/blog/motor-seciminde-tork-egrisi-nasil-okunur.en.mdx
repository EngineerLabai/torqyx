## What Does the Torque-Speed Characteristic Curve Tell You?

An electric motor's torque-speed curve graphically displays the exact rotational torque ($T$ in $\text{N}\cdot\text{m}$) the machine can produce across its entire rotational velocity range ($n$ in $\text{rpm}$ or $\omega$ in $\text{rad/s}$).

Selecting an electric motor based solely on nameplate mechanical power (e.g., "7.5 kW") without inspecting its torque curve frequently leads to machine startup failures, drive trips, or motor burnout. A motor capable of delivering 7.5 kW at 3000 rpm produces only half the torque of a 7.5 kW motor rated at 1500 rpm ($P = T \cdot \omega$). Furthermore, the transient torque available during initial breakaway and acceleration differs vastly across motor architectures (induction, brushless DC, synchronous reluctance, or servo).

## Key Torque Benchmarks on Induction & Servo Curves

When evaluating manufacturer datasheets (governed by IEC 60034-1 or NEMA MG 1), identify these five critical points:

1. **Locked-Rotor Torque ($T_L$ or Starting Torque):** The torque the motor develops at zero speed ($n = 0$) when rated voltage and frequency are applied. If $T_L$ is lower than static system stiction (breakaway torque), the machine will not turn.
2. **Pull-Up Torque ($T_u$):** The minimum torque developed between standstill and the breakdown point. In induction motors, this dip can stall high-inertia machinery during spin-up.
3. **Breakdown / Pull-Out Torque ($T_b$):** The maximum transient torque the motor can generate at operating speed before immediately stalling. Typically $2.0$ to $3.5$ times rated torque in AC induction motors.
4. **Rated Continuous Torque ($T_N$ - S1 Duty):** The steady-state torque the motor can deliver continuously without exceeding thermal insulation class limits (e.g., Class F/B rise).
5. **Peak Intermittent Torque ($T_{\text{peak}}$ - S3 Duty):** Available on servo and inverter-duty motors for short accelerations (e.g., $200\text{--}300\%$ of $T_N$), strictly bounded by drive current limits and rotor demagnetization thresholds.

## Matching the Load Profile to the Motor Curve

A drive system operates reliably only when the load torque curve lies comfortably within the motor's permissible operating envelope across all operating points:

- **Quadratic Torque Loads (Centrifugal Fans & Pumps):** Torque scales with the square of speed ($T \propto n^2$). Breakaway demand is low, but continuous demand surges at full operating speed.
- **Constant Torque Loads (Conveyors, Extruders, Positive Displacement Pumps):** Friction requires consistent torque from 1 rpm to rated speed. Adequate low-speed cooling (forced cooling fan) is vital when operated via VFDs.
- **High Breakaway / High Inertia Loads (Centrifuges, Crushers, Loaded Ball Mills):** Starting torque demand spikes drastically at standstill. Locked-rotor torque and acceleration margins dominate selection.

## Factoring Rotational Inertia and Acceleration Demands

Motors do not just overcome steady friction; they must accelerate rotating masses to target cycle times. Total required dynamic motor torque during acceleration ($T_{\text{mot}}$) is:

$$T_{\text{mot}} = T_{\text{load}} + T_{\text{acc}} = T_{\text{load}} + J_{\text{total}} \cdot \frac{d\omega}{dt} = T_{\text{load}} + J_{\text{total}} \cdot \frac{2\pi \cdot \Delta n}{60 \cdot t_{\text{acc}}}$$

Where:
- $J_{\text{total}} = J_{\text{motor}} + J_{\text{load, reflected}}$ is total mass moment of inertia ($\text{kg}\cdot\text{m}^2$).
- $t_{\text{acc}}$ is desired acceleration time in seconds.

If the load inertia reflected to the motor shaft is excessively large relative to motor rotor inertia ($J_{\text{load}} / J_{\text{motor}} > 10$ for servos, or $>20$ for general industrial machines), the motor spends excessive time in high-current slip regions, triggering thermal overload trips.

## VFD (Inverter Duty) Considerations: Constant Torque vs. Constant Power

When controlled by a Variable Frequency Drive:
- **Base Speed Region ($0$ to $f_{\text{base}}$, e.g., $0\text{--}50\text{ Hz}$):** Operates under constant volts-per-hertz ($V/f$), maintaining full rated continuous torque $T_N$.
- **Field Weakening Region (Above $f_{\text{base}}$, e.g., $>50\text{ Hz}$):** Voltage cannot exceed mains supply; motor flux weakens, causing available torque to decay inversely with speed ($T \propto 1/n$) while power remains constant. Sizing a machine above base speed must account for this torque drop-off.
