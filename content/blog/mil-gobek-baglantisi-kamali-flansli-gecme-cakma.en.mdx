## Connection Choice Affects More Than Pure Torque

The mechanical interface between a rotating shaft and its hub (gear, pulley, coupling, or flywheel) is one of the most critical load paths in any powertrain. While all connections transmit rotary torque ($T$), they behave very differently in terms of:
- **Stress concentrations and notch fatigue,**
- **Shaft concentricity and balancing runout,**
- **Assembly and field disassembly effort,**
- **Dynamic shock and reversing torque tolerance.**

Choosing an oversized shaft diameter does not protect a machine if the hub connection method introduces an aggressive notch or is prone to fretting fatigue.

## Keyed Connections (DIN 6885): Ubiquitous and Serviceable

Parallel keys per DIN 6885-1 are the industrial standard for general power transmission. Torque is transmitted by form-fit contact: the key experiences transverse shear stress ($\tau$) while its side flanks exert surface compressive pressure ($p$) against the shaft and hub keyways.

### Advantages:
- Highly economical and standardized across all metric diameters.
- Straightforward disassembly using standard pullers.

### Limitations:
- **Severe Stress Concentration:** Milling a keyseat introduces high geometric notch factors ($\alpha_k \approx 2.0 - 3.0$), drastically reducing the shaft's torsional and bending fatigue endurance limit.
- **Backlash & Fretting under Reversals:** Under alternating or pulsating torque, slight clearance allows micro-motion, leading to fretting corrosion, peening, and eventual key shearing.
- **Zero Axial Retention:** Keys do not locate hubs axially; external shaft shoulders, set screws, or retaining rings (DIN 471) are mandatory.

## Flanged Connections: Extreme Torque and Controlled Serviceability

In heavy machinery, marine propulsion, and large turbomachinery, torque is transferred through bolted flange couplings. Torque transmission occurs via friction generated by heavy bolt clamping preload, supplemented in extreme designs by precision fitted reamed bolts operating in shear.

### Advantages:
- Exceptional torque capacity scalable via pitch diameter and bolt count.
- Eliminates shaft keyway stress risers.
- Easy axial and radial decoupling during maintenance without disturbing bearings.

### Limitations:
- Bulkier envelope requiring significant radial clearance.
- Precise flange perpendicularity and face runout machining required to avoid shaft misalignment vibrations.

## Interference Fits (Press-Fit & Shrink-Fit): Notch-Free and Concentric

In interference fits (governed by DIN 7190), the hub bore is machined smaller than the shaft outer diameter (e.g., ISO fit $p6/H7$ or $s6/H7$). When assembled via hydraulic pressing or thermal shrink-fitting (heating the hub / freezing the shaft), the elastic interference generates uniform radial contact pressure ($p_c$). Torque is transmitted purely by frictional shear stress ($\tau = \mu \cdot p_c$).

### Advantages:
- **Zero Keyways:** Eliminates keyway stress risers, preserving the maximum fatigue endurance limit of the solid shaft.
- **Superior Concentricity:** Self-centering action delivers near-zero radial runout, essential for high-speed rotors ($>3000\text{ rpm}$).
- **Immunity to Backlash:** Perfect for dynamic reversing and high shock cycles.

### Limitations:
- Demands tight machining tolerances (ISO IT6).
- Disassembly requires specialized induction heaters, hydraulic oil injection grooves, or destructive pulling.

| Feature | Keyed Joint (DIN 6885) | Bolted Flange Joint | Interference Fit (DIN 7190) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Transmission Mechanism** | Form-fit (shear & surface contact) | Friction via bolt preload / shear | Friction via radial contact pressure |
| **Fatigue Notch Effect** | High ($\alpha_k \approx 2.5$) | Low to none | Low (fretting at hub edge) |
| **Reversing Torque Resistance** | Moderate to poor (fretting) | Excellent | Outstanding (zero backlash) |
| **Concentricity & Runout** | Moderate | High | Excellent (self-centering) |
| **Disassembly Ease** | Simple (standard tools) | Very simple (unbolt) | Difficult (hydraulic/thermal gear) |
| **Relative Manufacturing Cost** | Low | High | Medium (tight tolerances) |

## Sizing Checks and Design Verification

For verified engineering sizing:
1. **Keyed Joints:** Check flank surface pressure $p = \frac{2 \cdot T}{d \cdot (h - t_1) \cdot l_e} \le p_{\text{perm}}$, where $l_e$ is effective key length.
2. **Press-Fits:** Check that interference produces sufficient holding torque $T_{\text{slip}} = \pi \cdot d^2 \cdot l \cdot \mu \cdot p_c / 2 \ge S \cdot T_{\text{peak}}$, while ensuring hub tangential tensile stresses do not exceed material yield: $\sigma_{t,\max} \le R_{p0.2} / S$.
3. **Flanged Joints:** Verify bolt clamp force against interface friction: $T = \mu \cdot F_M \cdot r_{\text{friction}} \cdot z_{\text{bolts}}$.
