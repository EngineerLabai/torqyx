## Torque and Power Are Not the Same

Torque is the rotational turning moment applied to a shaft, measured in newton-meters (Nm) or pound-feet (lb-ft). Mechanical power is the time rate at which work is performed or energy is transmitted, expressed in watts (W), kilowatts (kW), or horsepower (hp). A machine delivering massive torque does not inherently deliver high power unless it turns at an appreciable rotational velocity. 

Power is the mathematical product of torque and angular velocity:

$$P = T \cdot \omega$$

In SI engineering practice with rotational speed in revolutions per minute (rpm), this relationship is expressed as:

$$P (\text{kW}) = \frac{T (\text{Nm}) \cdot n (\text{rpm})}{9550}$$

This formula allows mechanical engineers to derive required power when torque and operating speed are known, or calculate available shaft torque when motor power and speed ratings are provided.

## Why Rotational Speed Changes the Outcome

A drive shaft delivering 100 Nm at 500 rpm performs work at a drastically different rate than a shaft transmitting that same 100 Nm at 3,000 rpm. The second shaft completes six times as many revolutions per second, requiring six times more input power ($P \approx 31.4\text{ kW}$ versus $5.23\text{ kW}$). 

Conversely, at constant input power, reducing shaft speed through a mechanical transmission multiplies available output torque. This explains the function of industrial speed reducers: by stepping down angular velocity, they amplify usable output torque to overcome high starting resistances, such as crane winches, heavy conveyors, or extruder screws.

## Sizing Motors: Evaluating Both Parameters Together

Selecting an electric motor solely on nominal nameplate power ($kW$) introduces operational risk. Mechanical designers must analyze starting torque, breakdown torque, continuous duty cycle, thermal dissipation, and driven inertia ($J$). For example, a standard 7.5 kW 4-pole motor operating at 1,460 rpm provides a continuous shaft torque of approximately 49 Nm. Sizing a gearbox with a 30:1 reduction yields nearly 1,350 Nm of usable torque at ~48 rpm (accounting for mechanical efficiency). 

Evaluating torque-speed curves across all transient operating states guarantees that equipment starts reliably without tripping over-current protection or stalling under peak resistance.
