## Why Is the K Factor Used? The Short-Form Torque Equation

In industrial fastener assembly, engineers and assembly technicians frequently calculate tightening torque using the simplified "short-form" empirical formula:

$$T = K \cdot F_M \cdot d$$

Where:
- $T$ is the applied tightening torque ($\text{N}\cdot\text{m}$).
- $K$ is the dimensionless **torque coefficient** or **nut factor**.
- $F_M$ is the target clamp preload force ($\text{N}$).
- $d$ is the nominal major thread diameter ($\text{m}$).

While elegant in its simplicity, the nut factor $K$ is often misunderstood as a simple synonym for the coefficient of friction ($\mu$). In reality, $K$ is an empirical catch-all constant that bundles thread pitch angle, thread flank friction ($\mu_{th}$), underhead/nut bearing face friction ($\mu_b$), washer interaction, surface roughness, and plating compliance into a single dimensionless number.

## Where Does Applied Tightening Torque Really Go?

When a mechanic applies $100\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}$ to a standard metric bolt:
- **Only $\approx 10\% - 15\%$** of the energy converts into useful axial tension ($F_M$) that elastically stretches the bolt shank and clamps the joint together.
- **$\approx 40\% - 45\%$** is dissipated as sliding friction between the mating male and female thread flanks.
- **$\approx 45\% - 50\%$** is dissipated as rotational friction between the rotating bolt head (or nut) underside and the stationary washer/flange surface.

Because nearly $90\%$ of the torque is consumed entirely by friction, even a minor change in surface chemistry, lubricant, or plating finish dramatically swings the resulting clamp force.

## Typical K-Factor Values Across Fastener Finishes

The table below summarizes empirical $K$-factor ranges commonly observed in industrial assemblies:

| Fastener Condition & Plating | Typical K-Factor Range | Assembly Implications |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **As-Received Plain Black Steel (Dry)** | $0.20 - 0.30$ | High scatter; wide variance in clamping force |
| **Light Machine Oil (As-Received)** | $0.15 - 0.18$ | Standard general engineering benchmark |
| **Electro-Zinc Plated (Dry / Unwaxed)** | $0.22 - 0.35$ | Risk of thread galling and severe stick-slip |
| **Zinc Plated with Wax Sealant / Lubricant** | $0.12 - 0.15$ | High consistency for automated multi-spindle assembly |
| **Cadmium Plated** | $0.12 - 0.14$ | Excellent lubricity (restricted due to environmental toxicity) |
| **Stainless Steel (A2 / A4, Dry)** | $0.25 - 0.35$ | Extreme risk of galling and cold welding |
| **Anti-Seize Moly / PTFE Paste** | $0.09 - 0.12$ | Low friction; risk of overstretching if torque is not reduced |

## The Critical Risk: Applying Dry Torque to Lubricated Bolts

The greatest practical danger in field assembly is applying a handbook "dry" tightening torque to a fastener that has been coated with anti-seize paste, grease, or threadlocker.

Consider an M16 Grade 8.8 bolt ($d = 16\text{ mm}$):
- Specified dry torque ($K = 0.20$): $T \approx 175\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}$ to achieve a target preload of $F_M \approx 54.7\text{ kN}$.
- If a technician applies moly paste ($K = 0.11$) but continues tightening to $175\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}$:

$$F_M = \frac{T}{K \cdot d} = \frac{175}{0.11 \cdot 0.016} \approx 99.4\text{ kN}$$

The actual clamping force surges to $99.4\text{ kN}$—far exceeding the bolt's yield strength ($R_{p0.2} \approx 640\text{ MPa}$, yield force $\approx 100\text{ kN}$). The fastener stretches past its elastic limit and strips threads or snaps during assembly.

## Rigorous Calculation: The VDI 2230 Approach

For mission-critical bolted connections (pressure containment, rotating machinery, aerospace mounts), the simplified $K$-factor equation is replaced by the rigorous VDI 2230 thread physics formulation:

$$T = F_M \left( \frac{d_2}{2} \tan(\phi + \rho') + \frac{D_m}{2} \mu_b \right)$$

Where:
- $d_2$ is thread pitch diameter.
- $\phi$ is thread lead angle ($\tan\phi = P / (\pi \cdot d_2)$).
- $\rho'$ is friction angle on thread flanks ($\tan\rho' = \mu_{th} / \cos(\alpha/2)$).
- $D_m$ is mean diameter of the bolt head contact face.
- $\mu_b$ is friction coefficient under the bolt head or nut.
