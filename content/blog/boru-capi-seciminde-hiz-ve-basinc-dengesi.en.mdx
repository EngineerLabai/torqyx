## Pipe sizing is fundamentally an economic and hydraulic balance

It is physically possible to force any volumetric flow rate ($Q$) through an undersized pipe, provided a sufficiently large pump or compressor is installed. However, as fluid velocity ($v$) climbs, friction head loss spikes with the square of velocity ($v^2$), driving up acoustic noise, pipe wall erosion, and pump electric power consumption. Conversely, specifying an oversized pipe inflates upfront capital procurement costs, pipe rack structural dead weight, and insulation expenses, while potentially causing particulate settling in slurry systems.

Optimal pipe sizing is the balance point between upfront capital investment (CAPEX) and recurring lifecycle pumping energy costs (OPEX). The core hydraulic relationship begins with the volumetric continuity equation:
$$v = \frac{4 \cdot Q}{\pi \cdot D_i^2}$$
Given a fixed flow rate $Q$, flow velocity varies inversely with the square of the internal bore diameter ($D_i$). Frictional pressure drop is subsequently evaluated using the Darcy-Weisbach formulation:
$$\Delta P = f \cdot \frac{L}{D_i} \cdot \frac{\rho \cdot v^2}{2}$$

## Establishing velocity criteria across industrial applications

There is no single universal flow velocity suitable for all fluid transport duties. Acceptable velocity windows are dictated by fluid phase, particulate content, cavitation risk, acoustic thresholds, and fluid rheology:
- **Centrifugal Pump Suction Lines:** $v = 0.8 - 1.2\text{ m/s}$ (Mandatory to minimize suction head loss and guarantee positive NPSH margins to prevent impeller cavitation).
- **Pump Discharge & Water Distribution:** $v = 1.5 - 2.5\text{ m/s}$ (Economic balance between pump head and pipe diameter).
- **Compressed Air & Process Gases:** $v = 6.0 - 15.0\text{ m/s}$ (High gas velocities create acoustic noise and drag water droplets into pneumatic tools).
- **Hydraulic Oil High-Pressure Lines:** $v = 3.0 - 5.0\text{ m/s}$ (Viscous oil dissipation generates thermal overheating).
- **Boiler Steam Lines:** $v = 20.0 - 35.0\text{ m/s}$ for dry saturated steam, up to $45.0\text{ m/s}$ for superheated steam.

## The true energy cost of excessive pressure drop

Because straight pipe frictional pressure drop scales with $v^2 / D_i$—which mathematically equates to $1 / D_i^5$ for a constant volumetric flow rate—dropping pipe size from DN100 ($100\text{ mm}$) to DN80 ($80\text{ mm}$) increases friction head loss by approximately **300%**! 

The continuous hydraulic power dissipated into fluid heat is given by:
$$P_{hydraulic} = Q \cdot \Delta P = \frac{\rho \cdot g \cdot Q \cdot H}{1000} \quad [\text{kW}]$$
In continuous 24/7 industrial service ($8000\text{ hours/year}$), an undersized line quickly burns more electrical power in its first two years of operation than the total upfront cost savings realized by purchasing the smaller pipe.

## Minor fittings and actual internal diameter ($D_i$)

A common design error is using nominal pipe size (NPS / DN) in velocity and pressure drop calculations instead of actual internal bore diameter ($D_i$). Schedule wall thickness variations (e.g., Schedule 40 vs Schedule 80 or Schedule 160) substantially reduce internal flow area. For example, a 2-inch Schedule 40 pipe has an inside diameter of $52.5\text{ mm}$, whereas Schedule 80 drops to $49.3\text{ mm}$, increasing velocity and friction by over 15%.

Furthermore, compact mechanical skids containing multiple elbows, check valves, basket strainers, and control valves frequently experience minor fittings losses ($\sum K \cdot \frac{\rho v^2}{2}$) that far exceed straight pipe skin friction.

## Systematic pipe sizing methodology

1. **Quantify Normal and Peak Volumetric Flow ($Q$):** Base calculations on continuous nominal flow while verifying that peak surge flows do not exceed maximum velocity caps.
2. **Retrieve Fluid Properties at Operating Temperature:** Determine fluid density ($\rho$) and dynamic viscosity ($\mu$).
3. **Select Preliminary Target Velocity:** Choose standard target velocities from industry codes (e.g., $1.8\text{ m/s}$ for liquid discharge).
4. **Calculate Theoretical Bore and Select Standard Schedule:** Compute $D_{calc} = \sqrt{\frac{4Q}{\pi v}}$ and select the nearest standard commercial internal diameter.
5. **Evaluate Total System Pressure Drop:** Sum straight pipe Darcy-Weisbach loss plus minor fitting resistance ($K$) values.
6. **Calculate Pump Power and Perform Lifecycle Cost Analysis:** Compare annual kWh energy consumption against pipe procurement and support costs.
