<Callout type="info" title="Engineering Usage Note">
This guide provides practical methodologies to verify rotor unbalance and evaluate vibration severity in electric motors, pumps, fans, and turbomachinery in compliance with ISO 21940-11 and ISO 20816-1. For high-speed critical machinery, modal analysis and dual-plane dynamic balancing are mandatory.
</Callout>

## Problem / Objective

In rotating machinery, any offset between the center of mass and the geometric axis of rotation generates centrifugal forces proportional to the square of rotational speed (`F_c propto ω^2`). Left unmitigated, unbalance forces cause:
- Rapid depletion of bearing L10 life and premature fatigue spalling,
- Bolt loosening, keyway fretting, and structural fatigue cracking at foundation supports,
- Destructive resonance and catastrophic shaft deflection when forcing frequencies align with system natural frequencies.

## Assumptions

- The rotor behaves as a rigid body operating safely below its first critical bending speed (`n_operating &lt; 0.70  *  n_critical`).
- Bearing housings and support structures provide symmetric, linear stiffness characteristics.
- Vibration velocity is measured as RMS (`v_rms`) across the standard 10 Hz to 1000 Hz frequency range according to ISO 20816-1.

## Step by step method

1. **Select Balancing Mode Based on Rotor Geometry:**
   - **Single-Plane (Static Balancing):** For disc-shaped rotors where length-to-diameter ratio is small (`L/D &lt; 0.20` and `n &lt; 1000  rpm`), such as narrow pulleys, flywheels, and grinding wheels.
   - **Dual-Plane (Dynamic Balancing):** For elongated cylindrical rotors (`L/D  >=  0.20` or higher operating speeds), such as electric motor armatures, multi-stage pump impellers, and industrial fans.

2. **Determine the ISO 21940-11 Balance Quality Grade (`G`):**
   - **G6.3:** Standard industrial fans, gearbox shafts, agricultural equipment.
   - **G2.5:** Electric motors, machine tool spindles, gas/steam turbines, process compressors.
   - **G1.0:** High-precision grinding spindles, gyroscopes, turbocharger components.

3. **Calculate Permissible Residual Unbalance (`U_per`):**
   With total rotor mass `m` [`kg`] and angular velocity `ω = (2pi  *  n / 60)` [`rad/s`]:
   
`U_per = 1000  *  (G  *  m / ω)   [ g  *  mm ]`

   For dual-plane balancing, distribute `U_per` between the two correction planes based on distance from the center of gravity (`U_1 = U_2 = U_per / 2` for symmetric rotors).

4. **Quantify Unbalance Dynamic Centrifugal Force:**
   The cyclic force transmitted into radial bearings is:
   
`F_c = m_u  *  r  *  ω^2 = fracU_per1000  *  ω^2   [ N ]`


5. **Evaluate ISO 20816 Vibration Severity Zones:**
   Evaluate overall bearing housing RMS velocity (`v_rms` [`mm/s`]):
   - **Zone A (New Machine / Commissioning):** `v_rms &lt; 1.4  mm/s` (Medium industrial motors)
   - **Zone B (Unrestricted Long-Term Operation):** `1.4  <=  v_rms  <=  2.8  mm/s`
   - **Zone C (Restricted Operation / Plan Maintenance):** `2.8 &lt; v_rms  <=  4.5  mm/s`
   - **Zone D (Unacceptable / Immediate Shutdown):** `v_rms > 4.5  mm/s`

6. **Industrial Worked Example:**
   An industrial electric motor rotor with mass `m = 60  kg` operates at `n = 3000  rpm`. The target balancing grade is G2.5:
   - Angular velocity: `ω = (2pi  *  3000 / 60) = mathbf314.16  rad/s`
   - Maximum permissible residual unbalance:
     
`U_per = 1000  *  (2.5  *  60 / 314.16) = mathbf477.5  g  *  mm`

   - Shared between two symmetrical balancing correction planes:
     
`U_1 = U_2 = (477.5 / 2) = mathbf238.8  g  *  mm`

   - At a bolt correction radius of `r = 120  mm`, the permissible unbalance mass tolerance is:
     
`m_corr = (238.8 / 120) = mathbf1.99  grams`


## Common mistakes

- **Attempting Single-Plane Balancing on Long Rotors:** Ignoring couple unbalance across the shaft length leads to high vibration once operating speed is reached.
- **Confusing Foundation Soft-Foot or Misalignment with Rotor Unbalance:** Misalignment produces dominant 2X shaft rotational frequency peaks, whereas unbalance is strictly 1X; adding trial weights to misaligned machinery exacerbates bearing damage.
- **Dwell Time During Resonance Pass-Through:** Passing through shaft critical speed slowly creates huge vibration amplitudes; speed ramp-up through critical zones must be accelerated via VFDs.
- **Ignoring Thermal Bowing at Operational Temperature:** Turbines and hot gas fans balanced cold can bow when heated, creating substantial thermal unbalance during steady-state service.

## Vibration Frequency Analysis and Fault Diagnosis

Vibration data is only useful when interpreted in the frequency domain. Time-domain amplitude (mm/s RMS) confirms severity; the FFT spectrum identifies the source.

| Frequency Component | Typical Source | Diagnostic Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 1X (1 × RPM) | Residual unbalance, bow | Dominant 1X + phase shift → mass unbalance |
| 2X | Misalignment (parallel or angular) | High 2X with axial component → angular misalignment |
| 0.5X | Oil whirl in sleeve bearings | Subsynchronous instability in journal bearings |
| BPFO, BPFI | Outer/inner race defect | Ball Pass Frequency Outer/Inner = bearing damage |
| nX (high harmonics) | Gear mesh, looseness | Sidebands around gear mesh frequency → wear |

**Ball Pass Frequency Outer Race (BPFO):**
`BPFO = (Z / 2) * RPM/60 * (1 - Bd/Pd * cos(alpha))`

Where Z = number of rolling elements, Bd = ball diameter, Pd = pitch diameter, alpha = contact angle.

When amplitude at BPFO exceeds 3× background noise and sideband spacing matches shaft RPM, bearing replacement should be planned within 500 operating hours.

## Shaft Alignment Methods

Shaft misalignment accounts for approximately 50 % of all rotating machine failures. It causes elevated vibration at 2X RPM, increased bearing loads, and seal failures.

| Method | Accuracy | Equipment Cost | Recommended Application |
|---|---|---|---|
| Straight Edge | ±0.5 mm | Low | Pre-alignment check only |
| Dial Gauge (Rim-Face) | ±0.05 mm | Low | General industrial use |
| Reverse Dial Indicator | ±0.02 mm | Moderate | Long couplings, high RPM |
| Laser Alignment | ±0.005 mm | High | Precision machinery, turbines |

**Alignment targets (per ISO 10816, API 686):**
- Angular misalignment: < 0.05 mm/100 mm shaft length
- Parallel (offset) misalignment: < 0.05 mm for machines above 3000 RPM; < 0.10 mm below 1500 RPM

**Soft foot check:** Before final alignment, verify that all four machine feet contact the base simultaneously. A gap of even 0.1 mm under one foot creates a stress-induced distortion that invalidates the alignment. Use feeler gauges; correct with precision shims.

## Critical Speed and Rotor Dynamics

The critical speed is the rotational frequency at which the rotor's natural frequency coincides with the excitation frequency, causing resonance amplification.

For a simply supported Jeffcott rotor, the first critical speed is approximated by:

`n_krit = (30 / pi) * sqrt(g / f_static)`

Where:
- `g` = gravitational acceleration = 9.81 m/s²
- `f_static` = static deflection of the shaft under its own weight (m)
- `n_krit` = first critical speed (RPM)

**Example:** A shaft deflects 2 mm under self-weight → `n_krit = (30/π) × √(9.81/0.002) ≈ 2100 RPM`. Operating speed must stay at least 20 % below or 30 % above this value.

**Design rule:** For machines running above the first critical speed (supercritical operation), the rotor must pass through resonance during startup and shutdown. A sufficient damping ratio (ζ > 0.1) must be confirmed by rotor dynamics analysis before commissioning.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Which ISO balance quality grade (G class) should I use for my application?**
ISO 21940 defines G classes from G0.4 (precision grinders, gas turbines) to G100 (agricultural machinery crankshafts). For general industrial pumps and fans, G6.3 is standard. For electric motors at 3000 RPM, use G2.5. When in doubt, use a lower (stricter) G class — the cost of field balancing is always lower than an unplanned outage.

**Should I measure vibration in acceleration, velocity, or displacement?**
- **Displacement (µm):** Best for low-frequency vibration (<10 Hz); relevant for slow machinery and structural monitoring.
- **Velocity (mm/s):** The universal metric for rotating machinery (10–1000 Hz). ISO 10816 limits are given in mm/s RMS.
- **Acceleration (g or m/s²):** Best for high-frequency defects (>1000 Hz); used for bearing and gear fault detection.

**Can field balancing be performed without removing the rotor from the machine?**
Yes — single-plane and two-plane in-situ balancing are standard procedures. Measure initial vibration amplitude and phase (reference signal from a tachometer). Add a trial weight, remeasure, then calculate the correction weight using the influence coefficient method. Modern portable balancers perform this calculation automatically.
