<Callout type="info" title="Engineering Usage Note">
This guide is designed for sizing and validating piping and ductwork systems, pressure drops, and pump/fan operational points. For industrial plants and high-pressure networks, adhere to DIN EN 10255 and ISO 5167 standards.
</Callout>

## Problem / Objective

Sizing fluid transport systems based solely on volumetric flow rate ($Q$) is a common engineering mistake. Fluid viscosity, internal pipe diameter, velocity, and wall roughness together dictate the hydraulic friction. Failing to accurately identify the flow regime (laminar vs. turbulent) leads to:
- Underpowered pumps or fans that fail to deliver the design flow,
- Excessive flow velocities causing severe noise, pipe erosion, and water hammer hazards,
- Overly low velocities that induce particulate settling and degraded heat transfer coefficients.

## Assumptions

- The fluid exhibits Newtonian behavior and is treated as incompressible ($\rho \approx \text{constant}$).
- Fluid properties (density $\rho$ and dynamic viscosity $\mu$) are evaluated at the bulk operating temperature.
- Flow is fully developed and steady-state along the pipe or duct section.
- Non-circular ducts are handled via the hydraulic diameter ($D_h$) formulation.

## Step by step method

1. **Calculate Flow Area and Mean Velocity:**
   Given volumetric flow ($Q$ [$\text{m}^3\text{/s}$]) and pipe inside diameter ($D$ [$\text{m}$]):
   $$v = \frac{4 \cdot Q}{\pi \cdot D^2}$$

2. **Compute Hydraulic Diameter for Non-Circular Conduits:**
   For rectangular or non-circular cross-sections with flow area $A$ and wetted perimeter $P_{wetted}$:
   $$D_h = \frac{4 \cdot A}{P_{wetted}}$$

3. **Evaluate the Reynolds Number:**
   Determine the ratio of inertial to viscous forces:
   $$Re = \frac{\rho \cdot v \cdot D_h}{\mu} = \frac{v \cdot D_h}{\nu}$$
   - **$Re < 2300$:** Laminar flow regime (parabolic velocity profile).
   - **$2300 \le Re \le 4000$:** Transitional flow regime.
   - **$Re > 4000$:** Fully turbulent flow regime (flat velocity core with eddy dissipation).

4. **Determine the Darcy Friction Factor ($f$):**
   - In the laminar regime, friction is independent of surface roughness:
     $$f = \frac{64}{Re}$$
   - In the turbulent regime, calculate $f$ using the explicit Haaland approximation ($\varepsilon$: pipe roughness):
     $$\frac{1}{\sqrt{f}} \approx -1.8 \cdot \log_{10}\left[ \left(\frac{\varepsilon / D}{3.7}\right)^{1.11} + \frac{6.9}{Re} \right]$$

5. **Compute Straight Pipe Friction Loss (Darcy-Weisbach):**
   The major pressure drop along length $L$ is:
   $$\Delta P_{major} = f \cdot \frac{L}{D_h} \cdot \frac{\rho \cdot v^2}{2}$$

6. **Add Minor Losses from Fittings and Valves:**
   Sum the resistance coefficients ($K$) of all bends, valves, and transitions:
   $$\Delta P_{total} = \Delta P_{major} + \sum K \cdot \frac{\rho \cdot v^2}{2}$$

7. **Industrial Worked Example:**
   Water at 20 °C ($\rho = 998\text{ kg/m}^3$, $\mu = 1.002 \times 10^{-3}\text{ Pa}\cdot\text{s}$) flows at $Q = 10\text{ m}^3\text{/h}$ ($0.00278\text{ m}^3\text{/s}$) through a 50 m commercial steel pipe DN50 ($D = 53\text{ mm}$, $\varepsilon = 0.045\text{ mm}$):
   - Flow velocity: $v = \frac{4 \times 0.00278}{\pi \times 0.053^2} = \mathbf{1.26\text{ m/s}}$ *(Optimal liquid range: 1.0 - 2.0 m/s)*
   - Reynolds number: $Re = \frac{998 \times 1.26 \times 0.053}{0.001002} = \mathbf{66\,480}$ *(Turbulent)*
   - Relative roughness: $\varepsilon / D = 0.045 / 53 \approx 0.00085$
   - Haaland equation yields: $f \approx 0.0224$
   - Major head loss: $\Delta P_{major} = 0.0224 \times \frac{50}{0.053} \times \frac{998 \times 1.26^2}{2} = \mathbf{16\,740\text{ Pa}} \approx \mathbf{0.167\text{ bar}}$

## Common mistakes

- **Neglecting Hydraulic Diameter in Rectangular Ducting:** Taking an arithmetic average of width and height causes over 20% error in pressure drop prediction.
- **Ignoring Viscosity Shift with Temperature:** For oils and glycols, viscosity drops by an order of magnitude between 20 °C and 80 °C; cold start motor loads will trip breakers if unverified.
- **Exceeding Recommended Velocity Thresholds:** Velocity above 2.5 m/s in liquid lines produces pipe erosion and cavitation; suction lines must stay below 1.2 m/s to protect pump NPSH.
- **Overlooking Minor Component Losses:** In compact mechanical skids with multiple elbows, check valves, and strainers, minor losses frequently exceed straight pipe friction.
