## Problem / Objective

Wrong viscosity selection produces damage through four distinct mechanisms: insufficient film thickness (metal-to-metal contact → wear), excessive viscosity (pumping losses, heat accumulation), cold-start freezing or high flow resistance, and incorrect lubrication interval determination. ISO VG and SAE grades measure the same physical quantity but use different reference points and methodologies; attempting to interchange them directly invites systemic error.

This guide explains viscosity measurement systems, the correct selection method, and the most common mistakes.

## Assumptions

- The oil manufacturer's technical data sheet is available, with kinematic viscosity at 40°C and 100°C documented.
- The operating temperature range (minimum cold start and maximum steady-state) is known.
- The application type is defined: hydraulic system, gearbox, bearing, engine, slideway.
- Service interval or oil change cycle is established.
- The lubricant is used in an application that requires continuous film formation (elastohydrodynamic or hydrodynamic regime).

## Step by step method

### Step 1 — Understand kinematic viscosity measurement systems

Viscosity is expressed in three different units in engineering:

**Kinematic viscosity (ν):** in cSt (centistokes) or mm²/s. This is flow resistance normalized by the fluid's own density. ISO VG grades and ISO 3448 use this basis.

**Dynamic viscosity (μ):** in mPa·s (millipascal-seconds) or cP (centipoise). `μ = ν * ρ` (ρ = density in kg/m³).

**Viscosity index (VI):** A dimensionless number measuring how viscosity changes with temperature. Higher VI means more stable viscosity over a wide temperature range. Mineral oils: VI approximately 100; synthetic PAO: VI approximately 130–150; multigrade oils with VI improvers: VI greater than 150.

### Step 2 — Select ISO VG grade based on operating temperature

The ISO VG (Viscosity Grade) system is defined by ISO 3448 / DIN 51519. Each grade corresponds to a nominal kinematic viscosity at 40°C (±10% tolerance):

| ISO VG Grade | 40°C Viscosity (cSt) | Typical Application |
|---|---|---|
| VG 10 | 10 | Precision instruments, pneumatics |
| VG 22 | 22 | Textiles, small gears |
| VG 32 | 32 | Hydraulic systems (medium pressure) |
| VG 46 | 46 | Hydraulics, industrial gears |
| VG 68 | 68 | Heavy hydraulics, reducers |
| VG 100 | 100 | Industrial gearboxes |
| VG 150 | 150 | Large gearboxes, lead screws |
| VG 220 | 220 | Heavy-duty reducers |
| VG 320 | 320 | Very heavy gears, open gears |
| VG 460 | 460 | Low speed, very high load |

Selection path: determine the required viscosity at your operating temperature; then use the Walther viscosity-temperature equation or the manufacturer's graph to read the 40°C equivalent and select the nearest VG grade.

### Step 3 — Understand SAE grades and AGMA gear grades

**SAE Engine Oil Grades (SAE J300):**

SAE grades operate with a dual system:
- Winter (W) grades: `0W, 5W, 10W, 15W, 20W, 25W` — limit low-temperature viscosity (cP at –15°C or –35°C)
- Summer grades: `20, 30, 40, 50, 60` — limit kinematic viscosity at 100°C (cSt)
- Multigrade: such as `5W-30, 10W-40`; satisfies both cold and hot requirements

SAE engine oil grades must not be confused with ISO VG industrial oil grades. SAE 30 ≈ ISO VG 100 is an approximate equivalence only; test method, additive package, and application area are entirely different.

**AGMA Gear Oil Grades (AGMA 9005):**

AGMA grades are specific to industrial gearboxes:

| AGMA | Approximate ISO VG Equivalent |
|---|---|
| 0 | VG 46 |
| 1 | VG 68 |
| 2 | VG 100 |
| 3 | VG 150 |
| 4 | VG 220 |
| 5 | VG 320 |
| 6 | VG 460 |
| 7 | VG 680 |
| 8 | VG 1000 |

### Step 4 — Evaluate cold start and high-temperature film together

At cold start, oil fluidity is critical for pumping, acceleration, and initial loading. High viscosity at cold start:

- Increases pump pressure drop
- Delays oil reaching bearings (dry friction period)
- Contributes to high inrush current in electric motor-driven systems

Conversely, excessively low viscosity at operating temperature disrupts hydrodynamic film formation.

Practical path: check the oil's `ν` value at both the minimum cold start temperature and the maximum steady-state temperature. If a single viscosity grade cannot satisfy both extremes, a multigrade oil or synthetic formulation is required.

### Step 5 — Select a stock grade compatible with maintenance intervals

Final check in oil selection: the chosen grade must be available from current stock, suppliers, and rotation schedule. A grade that does not match existing inventory increases field confusion and wrong-oil-fill risk.

When standardizing oil grades across multiple machines, using the same grade for machines with overlapping performance requirements simplifies maintenance and reduces wrong-fill errors.

## Common mistakes

**1. Treating ISO VG 40°C as the operating temperature**

The reference point for ISO VG grades is 40°C. If the machine runs at 80°C, the actual viscosity at that temperature can drop far below the ISO VG number and film thickness becomes insufficient. Always verify actual viscosity at operating temperature.

**2. Directly substituting SAE engine oil grade for industrial oil**

SAE 30 engine oil ≠ ISO VG 100 industrial oil. Additive package, oxidative stability, foam resistance, and compatible material properties are entirely different. Engine oil must not go into a gearbox; gear oil must not go into an engine.

**3. Assuming higher viscosity always provides better protection**

Above a certain viscosity threshold, film thickness is sufficient; higher viscosity only increases pumping losses, heat accumulation, and energy consumption. In high-speed, low-load applications, excessive viscosity can contribute to foaming and cavitation.

**4. Mixing brands without compatibility check**

Even when two brands share the same VG grade and API category, their additive packages may conflict. The safest path: flush the system at oil change, or mix only per the same manufacturer's recommendation. Compatibility charts are generally available on manufacturer websites.

**5. Not adjusting oil change interval for ambient temperature**

High operating temperature accelerates oil oxidation and thermal degradation. Each 10°C increase approximately doubles the oxidative degradation rate (Arrhenius approximation). In overheating systems, the catalog oil change interval should be halved.

## Case summary — TORQYX Engineering Board, Fluids Desk

An industrial reducer was running on ISO VG 220 mineral oil. During winter months (ambient –5°C), oil pumping difficulty occurred, motor ramp-up time extended, and high-current alarms triggered.

Investigation: kinematic viscosity of the oil at –5°C reached approximately 2,200 cSt; pump design limit was 400 cSt. Solution: switched to ISO VG 68 PAO (polyalphaolefin) synthetic oil for winter operation. With PAO's VI approximately 145, approximately 280 cSt at –5°C and 18 cSt at 80°C were achieved; both extremes were satisfied. Start-up alarms ceased; annual energy consumption decreased by 4%.

## Frequently asked questions

### Question: What does ISO VG mean?

ISO VG (Viscosity Grade), per ISO 3448, specifies the nominal kinematic viscosity of an oil at 40°C. VG 46 means 46 ± 4.6 cSt kinematic viscosity at 40°C.

### Question: Is higher viscosity always better?

No. Optimum viscosity is the minimum value that provides adequate film thickness under operating conditions. Excessive viscosity causes energy loss, heat accumulation, and cold-start pumping problems.

### Question: Are SAE and ISO VG the same?

No. SAE engine oil grades are defined per J300; ISO VG industrial oils are defined per ISO 3448. Test methods, additive packages, and application areas differ. Substituting one for the other can cause incompatibility and failure.

### Question: When should a multigrade oil be selected?

When a single viscosity grade cannot satisfy both the cold-start extreme and the high operating temperature extreme in a system that sees a wide temperature range (seasonal ambient variation, cold start combined with high steady-state temperature).
