## Problem / Objective

In welded design, "more weld equals more safety" is usually wrong. Over-welding introduces distortion, residual stress, thermal degradation, and unnecessary cost. Under-welding creates fatigue crack, static failure, and non-conformance risk. This guide uses engineering evidence to make the selection between fillet weld and full-penetration (CJP) weld, and systematically separates the cost drivers.

Asking the right question: *In what load type does this joint operate, and where in the weld will that load create the most demand?*

## Assumptions

- Load path, direction, and magnitude are defined in advance.
- Material weldability is verified by carbon equivalent (CE) calculation.
- Weld access and position (flat, horizontal, vertical, overhead) are known.
- Inspection level is selected by application criticality (EN ISO 5817 quality level B, C, or D).
- If the design is under fatigue loading, life calculation is supported by an S-N curve.

## Step by step method

### Step 1 — Define load direction and effective weld length

The full weld length does not carry load. Effective weld length `l_eff` is what remains after start and stop craters are removed. Per EN 1993-1-8: `l_eff = l_total - 2 * a`, where a is throat thickness.

Load direction is critical:
- **Longitudinal welds (parallel load):** Weld stress is governed by shear. Formula: `tau_parallel = F / (a * l_eff)`
- **Transverse welds (perpendicular load):** The weld is subject to both normal and shear stress. Transverse resistance is approximately 50% higher than longitudinal, but plastic deformation capacity is lower.

### Step 2 — Compare fillet and full-penetration weld options

**Fillet Weld:**

- No joint preparation required (T-joint, lap joint, corner joint)
- Throat thickness `a = 0.707 * s` (s = leg size)
- Visual and dimensional inspection (VT) is sufficient in most cases
- Minimal post-weld work
- Exception: single-sided T-joint with limited access may be inadequate

**Full-Penetration (CJP) Weld:**

- Requires beveling, root preparation, and typically a backing bar
- The full parent material cross-section carries load; the joint throat is not the weak link
- Non-destructive testing (NDT) — UT, RT — is required
- Minimum 3–5× higher preparation + inspection cost

Selection rule:

| Criterion | Fillet Weld | Full Penetration |
|---|---|---|
| Static load, low stress | Adequate | Unnecessary |
| Fatigue load, high cycles | Limit-dependent | Preferred |
| Dynamic + tensile load | Careful evaluation | Preferred |
| Pressure vessel, bridge | Standard-mandated | Required |

### Step 3 — Calculate heat input and distortion risk

Heat input: `HI = (U * I * 60) / (v * 1000)` in kJ/mm (U: voltage V, I: current A, v: travel speed mm/min).

Distortion risk factors:
- Thin material (t less than 6 mm): high risk; small beads and symmetric sequence required
- Asymmetric cross-section: single-side heat converts to bending moment
- High HI: widens heat-affected zone (HAZ), increases martensite formation and crack risk

Distortion control tools: fixtures and clamps, symmetric weld sequence (alternating sides, balanced passes), preheat (recommended when CE exceeds 0.45).

### Step 4 — Set inspection method and acceptance criteria

| NDT Method | Application | Relative Cost |
|---|---|---|
| VT (Visual) | Every weld | 1× |
| PT (Penetrant) | Surface discontinuities | 2–3× |
| MT (Magnetic Particle) | Ferromagnetic surface + near-surface | 3–4× |
| UT (Ultrasonic) | Full-penetration internal | 5–8× |
| RT (Radiographic) | Full-pen, pipe, critical | 8–15× |

Acceptance criteria: EN ISO 5817 Level B (high quality), Level C (medium), Level D (low). Fatigue applications require at least Level C; pressure vessels require Level B.

### Step 5 — Break down cost into components

Weld cost has five elements:

1. **Weld metal material:** Proportional to deposited metal volume (kg comparison: fillet vs. full-penetration)
2. **Preparation cost:** Beveling, cleaning, backing bar; 2–4× higher for full penetration vs. fillet
3. **Welding time:** Single pass vs. multi-pass; full penetration takes 2–6× longer depending on joint type
4. **Inspection cost:** UT or RT; starting from VT baseline for fillet, 5–15× cost difference
5. **Rework cost:** Rejection rate × average repair time; can reach 20–30% of total cost when quality control is inadequate

## Common mistakes

**1. Treating the full geometric perimeter as effective weld length**

In a T-joint, the full plate edge may look welded, but corners and start-stop craters are ineffective. Calculating without `l_eff` overestimates capacity.

**2. Passing fatigue risk with a static-only check**

A weld that passes static analysis may fail the fatigue S-N curve. EN 1993-1-9 fatigue categories (FAT classes) must be selected based on weld geometry; an incorrect FAT class causes a 2–3× life error.

**3. Not planning fixtures for post-weld distortion**

In thin-plate structures, welding without fixtures ruins surface flatness. This leads to assembly problems, paint discontinuities, and increased fatigue stress.

**4. Always treating full penetration as superior**

Under simple static loading, full-penetration weld's extra cost and NDT requirement add no structural value. Choosing full penetration when fillet weld with correct throat thickness is adequate is not engineering conservatism; it is engineering waste.

**5. Ignoring the heat-affected zone (HAZ)**

In high-strength steels (S355 and above), hardness increase in the HAZ raises crack susceptibility. The preheat table (EN ISO 13916 or AWS D1.1 Annex) must be applied based on carbon equivalent.

## Case summary — TORQYX Engineering Board, Manufacturing Desk

An industrial platform structure in S275 steel had its connection nodes designed as fillet welds. The platform performed load-cycling transport work at 3–4 full load cycles per day. Annual inspection revealed micro-cracks at the fillet weld root.

Review: selected throat thickness was `a = 5 mm`. Fatigue life calculated at FAT class 63 (fillet weld with stress concentration included) yielded 120,000 cycles; actual service had reached this threshold within 3 years. Solution: throat increased to `a = 7 mm` and weld geometry optimized. Revised calculation predicted 420,000+ cycle life.

## Frequently asked questions

### Question: When is a fillet weld adequate?

When static load dominates, fatigue cycles are limited, and stress level is below the EN 1993-1-8 design resistance. Starting rule for throat: `a_min >= 0.7 * t_thinner_part`.

### Question: Is full-penetration welding always better?

No. It can improve structural integrity but brings preparation, inspection, and distortion cost with it. Under statically dominant loads, over-specifying hurts both budget and schedule.

### Question: What is the most common weld design mistake?

Not calculating effective weld length and load direction against the real assembly condition. Second most common: treating fatigue risk as passed by a static check alone.
