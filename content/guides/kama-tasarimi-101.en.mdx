<Callout type="info" title="Engineering Usage Note">
This guide details the strength verification of parallel shaft keys in accordance with DIN 6885-1 and ISO 773, as well as mechanical friction wedge locking mechanisms. For severe reversing torque or high-impact drives, interference press-fits, taper bushes, or involute splines are recommended.
</Callout>

## Problem / Objective

DIN 6885 parallel keys (feather keys) are the most prevalent mechanical coupling elements used to transmit torque between rotating shafts and gears, pulleys, or couplings. Likewise, taper wedges in tooling provide high clamping forces. Inadequate key sizing leads to:
- Excessive compressive surface pressure resulting in plastic crushing (bearing failure) and rotational backlash,
- Direct key shear failure along the shaft-hub interface under shock loads,
- Backwards slipping (loss of self-locking) in mechanical wedge clamps under dynamic vibration.

## Assumptions

- Key material is medium-carbon or alloy engineering steel (e.g., C45E or 16MnCr5) with yield strength equal to or greater than shaft and hub materials.
- Tangential force is uniformly distributed along the effective key engagement length (shaft torsional twist distortion is neglected).
- In round-ended keys (DIN 6885 Form A), the semicircular ends do not bear load; effective length is `l_k = l - b`.
- The friction coefficient (`μ`) between steel wedge surfaces is assumed constant for dry or lubricated contact.

## Step by step method

1. **Calculate Transmitted Tangential Force (`F_t`):**
   From shaft diameter `d` [`m`] and nominal operating torque `T` [`Nm`]:
   
`F_t = (2  *  T / d)`


2. **Select Standard Key Cross-Section (DIN 6885-1 / ISO 773):**
   Standard dimensions for key width (`b`), height (`h`), and shaft seat depth (`t_1`) are selected based on shaft diameter (`d`):
   - For `d = 38dots 44  mm`: `b = 12  mm`, `h = 8  mm`, `t_1 = 5.0  mm`, and hub depth `t_2 = 3.3  mm`.

3. **Verify Hub Surface Bearing/Crushing Stress (`σ_p`):**
   Because the hub is usually the softer material, check the compressive stress over the effective engagement length `l_k`:
   
`σ_p = (F_t / l_k  *  t_2)  <=  σ_p,allow`

   *(Permissible crushing limits: Grey Cast Iron GG-25: `60 - 80  MPa`; Cast Steel GS-45: `90 - 120  MPa`; Quenched & Tempered C45: `120 - 150  MPa`)*

4. **Verify Key Shear Stress (`τ_k`):**
   Check shear across the transverse cross-section:
   
`τ_k = (F_t / b  *  l_k)  <=  τ_allow  ≈  0.6  *  R_e / S_f`


5. **Verify Wedge Clamping Angle and Self-Locking Condition:**
   In inclined mechanical clamping wedges, self-locking occurs when the wedge taper angle `α` satisfies:
   
`tan(α)  <=  μ implies α  <=  arctan(μ)`

   *(Lubricated steel: `μ  ≈  0.10 implies α  <=  5.7^circ`; Dry clean steel: `μ  ≈  0.15 implies α  <=  8.5^circ`)*

6. **Industrial Worked Example:**
   A drive shaft with `d = 40  mm` transmits `T = 300  Nm` torque to a cast steel GS-45 hub. A DIN 6885 Form A key is specified (`b = 12  mm`, `h = 8  mm`, `t_2 = 3.3  mm`, total length `l = 50  mm`):
   - Tangential force: `F_t = (2  *  300 / 0.040) = mathbf15,000  N`
   - Effective contact length: `l_k = 50 - 12 = mathbf38  mm`
   - Compressive bearing stress:
     
`σ_p = (15,000 / 38  *  3.3) = mathbf119.6  MPa   <=  σ_p,allow  ≈  120  MPa     (Safe)`

   - Key shear stress:
     
`τ_k = (15,000 / 12  *  38) = mathbf32.9  MPa  ll τ_allow  ≈  75  MPa     (High Safety Factor)`


## Common mistakes

- **Using Total Length for Rounded-End Form A Keys:** Semicircular ends do not carry tangential load; using `l` instead of `l_k = l - b` underestimates bearing stress by 20% to 30%.
- **Ignoring Application Peak Torque (`K_A`):** In reciprocating compressors or press drives, dynamic shock multipliers range from 1.5 to 2.5; calculations must evaluate peak torque.
- **Overlooking Keyway Stress Concentration:** Machining a keyseat into a rotating shaft introduces a severe stress concentration factor (`K_t  ≈  2.5 - 3.0`) in fatigue bending/torsion.
- **Insufficient Hub Wall Thickness:** The radial wall thickness of the hub above the keyway must be at least `1.5  *  h` to avoid fatigue bursting.

## Spline, Taper Key, and Polygon Alternatives

Parallel keys are limited in torque capacity by the shear area of the key and the bearing stress on the keyway walls. For higher torques, the following alternatives should be evaluated:

**Involute Splines (DIN 5480 / ANSI B92.1):** Multiple teeth distribute the load across a large contact area. Torque capacity is 3–5× higher than a single key of equivalent shaft diameter. Used in gearboxes, automotive driveshafts, and machine tool spindles. Requires precise hobbing or broaching — not suitable for job-shop machining.

**Taper Key and Gib Head Key (DIN 6883/6884):** The taper creates a friction-based clamp between hub and shaft. Self-locking under torque reversal — no fastener required. Limited to low-speed, moderate-torque applications. Difficult to achieve repeatable assembly position.

**Polygon Profiles (P3G, DIN 32711):** Three-lobe polygon shafts use geometric interference to transmit torque without any separate key. Zero keyway stress concentration. Used in wood-working machinery, hydraulic pump drives, and high-speed spindles. Requires a specialized grinding process; not interchangeable with standard round bores.

| Feature | Parallel Key | Involute Spline | Taper Key | Polygon P3G |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Torque capacity | Moderate | High | Moderate | High |
| Axial positioning | Via retaining ring | Via snap ring/nut | Fixed | Via clamp nut |
| Keyway stress conc. | High (Kt ≈ 1.6–2.5) | Low | Low | Negligible |
| Machining complexity | Low | High | Low | Very High |

## Stress Concentration and Fatigue Factor

The keyway creates a significant stress concentration at the shaft surface. The theoretical stress concentration factor Kt depends on the ratio of keyway radius to shaft diameter:

| r/d Ratio | Kt (Bending) | Kt (Torsion) |
|---|---|---|
| 0.005 | 2.50 | 2.20 |
| 0.010 | 2.20 | 1.95 |
| 0.020 | 1.85 | 1.70 |
| 0.040 | 1.60 | 1.50 |

The fatigue notch factor Kf accounts for material notch sensitivity:

`Kf = 1 + q * (Kt - 1)`

Where q is the notch sensitivity index (0 to 1), dependent on material ultimate tensile strength and notch radius. For hardened steel (Su > 900 MPa), q ≈ 0.9, meaning the full Kt value must be considered. For mild steel (Su < 500 MPa), q ≈ 0.6 — the actual notch effect is reduced because the material redistributes stress locally.

**Practical implication:** A machined keyway reduces the shaft fatigue life by a factor of 2–4 compared to a plain shaft under reversed bending. If the shaft experiences combined bending and torsion at the keyway location, use the von Mises equivalent stress criterion with full Kf applied to both components.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does a key alone transmit torque, or does the shaft-hub interference fit also contribute?**
In practice, both contribute. For a standard H7/k6 (transition fit) or H7/m6 assembly, the friction between shaft and hub transfers a portion of the torque — reducing the key's load. However, design codes (DIN 6885, ISO 773) treat the key as carrying 100 % of the torque, with the interference fit providing only axial retention. This conservative approach accounts for fretting wear and fit relaxation over time.

**What keyway tolerance class should I specify for CNC machining?**
DIN 6885 specifies keyway width tolerances as JS9 (symmetric, ±IT9/2) for loose-fit keys and P9 for interference-fit keys. For general shaft-hub assemblies, use: shaft keyway = N9, hub keyway = JS9. For precision fits where backlash must be minimized, specify H9/n9 and lap the key to fit.

**What causes fretting corrosion at key-keyway interfaces and how can it be prevented?**
Fretting occurs when micro-slip (< 0.1 mm) takes place under cyclic loading between mating surfaces. The slip causes oxidation debris (brown powder, Fe2O3) and surface pitting, which initiates fatigue cracks. Prevention: use anti-fretting paste (molybdenum disulfide or copper-based), apply interference press fit alongside the key, minimize clearance in key-keyway fit, or switch to an involute spline.
