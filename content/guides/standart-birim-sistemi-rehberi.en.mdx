## Problem / Objective

A unit error is the most common path from a correct formula to a wrong answer. Engineering history offers concrete examples: in 1999, the Mars Climate Orbiter was lost due to a unit mismatch between N·s and lbf·s between NASA and its contractor. Closer examples: a designer forgetting that N/mm² and MPa are equivalent, a team confusing kPa and bar in internal communication, or a spreadsheet blending mm and m in the same diameter calculation.

This guide systematically organizes unit management in engineering calculations step by step and documents the most common traps.

## Assumptions

- The project's primary unit system is decided at the start (preferably SI).
- Suppliers, clients, or standards may use a different unit system.
- Calculation templates explicitly show units alongside input values.
- Report output includes both input and result units side by side.
- The rounding policy is decided and documented at the start of the project.

## Step by step method

### Step 1 — Lock the project unit set

The unit set to be used in an engineering project must be established in writing at the start and shared with all relevant parties. Typical SI-based mechanical engineering unit set:

| Quantity | Unit | Symbol |
|---|---|---|
| Length | millimetre | mm |
| Mass | kilogram | kg |
| Force | Newton | N |
| Pressure / Stress | Megapascal | MPa |
| Temperature | Celsius | °C |
| Torque | Newton-metre | N·m |
| Power | Kilowatt | kW |
| Energy | Joule | J |

Fixing this set at the top of the project notebook or calculation template reduces downstream confusion to near zero.

### Step 2 — Record incoming data with its source unit

Data from suppliers or clients may arrive in a different unit system. The source unit must be visible on the document before conversion is performed. Example:

- Supplier data sheet: `Flow capacity: 12 GPM` → Record: `12 GPM (source), conversion: 0.758 L/s`
- Standard pressure value: `150 PSI` → `1,034 kPa` or `10.34 bar`

Practical rule: note the source value and unit next to every converted value; add a source document reference where needed.

### Step 3 — Convert before the calculation and set the rounding level

Conversion must be performed before entering the formula, not inside it. Intermediate rounding shifts the result; retain full precision throughout.

Frequently used conversion factors:

| Conversion | Factor |
|---|---|
| 1 inch → mm | 25.4 |
| 1 ft → m | 0.3048 |
| 1 lbf → N | 4.4482 |
| 1 psi → kPa | 6.8948 |
| 1 bar → MPa | 0.1 |
| 1 HP (mechanical) → kW | 0.7457 |
| 1 ft·lbf → N·m | 1.3558 |
| 1 BTU/h → W | 0.2931 |

Rounding policy: retain a minimum of 5 significant figures in intermediate steps. In reports, significant figures must match measurement precision. Reporting a dimension measured to ±0.01 mm as 12.34567 mm implies misleading precision.

### Step 4 — Report results in the same unit family

If a calculation input is in mm and output is in N/mm², the report must preserve both units together. When the result feeds another calculation, expressing it in MPa is important for both unit consistency and error traceability.

Every table or spreadsheet must include a unit column or row; a dimensionless number in an engineering document is a dangerous omission.

### Step 5 — Perform a reverse-conversion sanity check for critical values

After reaching an important result, verify by reverse conversion:

Example: `σ = 285 MPa` → convert to N/mm²: `285 N/mm²` → convert to inch-pound: `285 / 0.00689476 ≈ 41,340 PSI`. Is this value within the expected range for the material's yield strength in inch-pound units? This cross-check catches gross-scale errors.

## Common mistakes

**1. Forgetting that N/mm² and MPa are equivalent**

1 N/mm² = 1 MPa is a mathematical fact. But confusing `E = 210 GPa` with `σ = 210 N/mm²` in a spreadsheet, or treating "210,000 N/mm²" and "210,000 MPa" as different magnitudes, is an observed real-world error.

**2. Mixing mm and m in the same formula**

Entering length in mm and force in N while reading area in m² is a common mistake. Dimensional analysis: `σ = F/A` → `N / mm²` = MPa is correct; `N / m²` = Pa means the same numerical value carries a factor of 1,000,000 difference.

**3. Skipping the factor of 1,000 between N·m and N·mm in torque calculations**

`T = 100 N·m = 100,000 N·mm`. In bolt preload formulas (VDI 2230), torque is typically entered in N·m; when shear pin or shaft calculations use N·mm, the factor of 1,000 is easily missed and produces an order-of-magnitude error.

**4. Forgetting the zero shift in temperature conversion**

°F → °C: `T_C = (T_F - 32) / 1.8`. The multiplier 5/9 is usually recalled correctly but forgetting to subtract 32 is the classic error. When thermodynamic calculations require absolute temperature (Kelvin or Rankine), note that 0°C = 273.15 K.

**5. Confusing pressure units (bar, atm, psi, kPa, Pa)**

1 bar ≠ 1 atm (1 atm = 1.01325 bar). 1 PSI = 6.8948 kPa ≈ 0.0689 bar. In industry, bar and kPa are frequently used together; which is displayed depends on the gauge or display device. Never trust a unitless pressure value from an ambiguous source.

## Real engineering incident — concrete consequences of unit confusion

Mars Climate Orbiter (1999): JPL's navigation software expected SI units while Lockheed Martin's software transmitted data in pound-force·seconds. Result: a \$327 million spacecraft deviated from orbit and was lost. The incident entered the record as the standard reference case for the critical importance of unit management in engineering documentation.

This example may seem extreme, but in smaller-scale projects, two engineers working independently or data from two different suppliers can produce the same unit clash with the same consequences.

## Frequently asked questions

### Question: Are N/mm² and MPa the same?

Yes, mathematically 1 N/mm² = 1 MPa. Unit confusion arises not from writing different numbers in two notations, but from shifting the magnitude scale during use.

### Question: Which unit system should be chosen for an engineering project?

Unless the client or standard mandates otherwise, SI (millimetre-based mechanical engineering set) is the best starting point. The majority of international standards are SI-based.

### Question: When should rounding be performed?

Retain raw values through intermediate steps; in the final report, significant figures must align with measurement precision. Premature rounding accumulates error.

### Question: How should I work with two suppliers using different unit systems?

Document the source unit for every incoming value; perform all conversions to the project unit set in a central conversion table that is approved and version-controlled. Cross-checking by two independent reviewers is recommended for critical values.
