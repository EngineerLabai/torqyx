<Callout type="info" title="Engineering Practice Note">
This guide addresses bending moments, shear stresses, and elastic deflection verifications for structural beams across crane girders, machine frames, and skid structures following classical Euler-Bernoulli theory and Eurocode 3 (EN 1993-1-1).
</Callout>

## Problem / Objective

In mechanical engineering and structural steel design, beams serve as primary load-bearing members across overhead cranes, support platforms, machine skids, and material handling systems. Sizing beam cross-sections solely against **yield stress limits (strength)** is a dangerous shortcut. Field reality demonstrates that even if a beam remains completely safe against plastic yield, excessive elastic deflection can render an entire mechanical system inoperable.

For example, excessive deflection across an overhead crane runway girder forces the hoist trolley to continuously climb an uphill slope toward runway supports, overloading travel motors and causing severe wheel flange scuffing. Similarly, micro-deflections within a precision CNC machine gantry destroy part machining tolerances. Consequently, modern structural standards enforce a dual-verification workflow: Ultimate Limit State (ULS - Strength) and Serviceability Limit State (SLS - Deflection).

## Assumptions

- Beam material is homogeneous, isotropic, and obeys Hooke's Law within its linear elastic regime.
- The beam is slender, with span-to-depth ratio `L / h > 10`, validating the Euler-Bernoulli plane section assumption.
- Deformations fall within small-deflection theory; axial force interactions are neglected.
- Lateral-torsional buckling (LTB) is prevented through proper compressive flange bracing.

## Step by step method

1. **Calculate Bending Stress (Navier's Formula):**
   Maximum normal stress in the cross-section is:
   `sigma = (M_b * y) / I = M_b / W_b`
   - `M_b`: Bending moment at the section (N*mm)
   - `I`: Second moment of area about the bending axis (mm^4)
   - `W_b`: Elastic section modulus (mm^3) (`W_b = I / y_max`)

2. **Apply Closed-Form Deflection Equations:**
   The governing differential equation is `E * I * (d^2 w / dx^2) = - M_b(x)`.
   - Simply supported beam with center point load (Span L, Load F):
     `f_max = (F * L^3) / (48 * E * I)` (at mid-span)
   - Simply supported beam with uniform distributed load (Load q):
     `f_max = (5 * q * L^4) / (384 * E * I)` (at mid-span)

3. **Verify Standard Deflection Limits (SLS):**
   - Floor and platform girders: `f_perm = L / 300`
   - Overhead crane bridge girders (EN 13001): `f_perm = L / 500` to `L / 1000`
   - Machine tool spindle beds: `f_perm = L / 1000` to `L / 3000`

4. **Real-World Case Study: 10-Ton Overhead Crane Girder:**
   - Span `L = 12000 mm`, wheel point load `F = 50 kN`.
   - Initial IPE 360 profile satisfied stress (`177.1 MPa < 235 MPa`), but sag reached `57.09 mm` (nearly triple the `L / 600 = 20 mm` legal limit).
   - Solution: Upgraded to an HEB 450 beam profile, reducing sag to `12.88 mm <= 20 mm` and eliminating motor overload stalls.

## Common mistakes

- **Confusing Moment of Inertia Axes (`I_x` vs `I_y`):** Installing wide-flange beams horizontally reduces flexural stiffness by up to 90 percent.
- **Neglecting Shear Distortion in Deep Beams:** For short stocky beams (`L / h < 5`), Timoshenko shear terms must be included.
- **Overlooking Lateral-Torsional Buckling:** Unbraced compression flanges can buckle sideways catastrophically before reaching nominal moment capacity.

## Section Selection: I-Beam, HEB, IPE, and Box Section

Selecting the correct beam cross-section involves balancing flexural stiffness (I), section modulus (W), weight, and fabrication cost. European standard sections are governed by EN 10365.

| Section | Weight (kg/m) | Ix (cm^4) | Wx (cm^3) | Typical Application |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| IPE 200 | 22.4 | 1943 | 194 | Light floor beams, purlins |
| HEB 200 | 61.3 | 7340 | 534 | Columns, heavy crane beams |
| HEA 200 | 42.3 | 3692 | 330 | Medium-duty beams |
| UB 203x102 (British) | 23.1 | 1818 | 179 | British standard; similar to IPE |
| Box Section (200x200x8) | 48.0 | 4985 | 499 | Torsion-critical, crane runway |

**Selection strategy:**
- **IPE:** High section modulus-to-weight ratio for bending; narrow flanges suit tight spaces but limit lateral stability.
- **HEB:** Very wide flanges provide superior lateral stability and column performance. Use for beams with high lateral-torsional buckling risk or when moment connections are required.
- **Box sections:** Highest torsional stiffness. Mandatory for overhead crane runway beams and structures with significant eccentric loading.

## Lateral Torsional Buckling (LTB)

A beam loaded in bending can fail by buckling laterally and twisting simultaneously before reaching the full plastic moment capacity. This mode — lateral torsional buckling — governs long spans with unbraced compression flanges.

The buckling moment resistance according to Eurocode 3 (EN 1993-1-1):

`M_b,Rd = chi_LT * W_y * f_y / gamma_M1`

Where:
- `chi_LT` = LTB reduction factor (from EN 1993-1-1 curves, depending on lambda_LT)
- `W_y` = plastic or elastic section modulus about major axis
- `f_y` = yield strength (MPa)
- `gamma_M1` = partial safety factor = 1.0 (EN) or 1.1 (recommended for welds)

**Non-dimensional slenderness:**
`lambda_LT = sqrt(W_y * f_y / M_cr)`

Where `M_cr` is the elastic critical moment, calculated from section properties and unbraced length. When `lambda_LT < 0.4`, LTB is not a governing mode (chi_LT = 1.0).

**Practical measures to prevent LTB:**
- Add lateral restraint (bracing) at compression flange at maximum L/6 spacing.
- Use compact sections (HEB, box) with high flange-to-web area ratio.
- Provide continuous concrete slab acting compositely — the slab restrains the compression flange laterally.

## Dynamic Loads and Impact Factors

Static beam design covers dead loads. For moving or dynamic loads, an impact factor (phi) amplifies the equivalent static force.

| Load Type | Impact Factor phi | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Pedestrian on floor beam | 1.0 (static) | EN 1991-1-1 |
| Overhead crane, class HC1 | 1.10 | EN 1991-3 |
| Overhead crane, class HC3 | 1.25–1.45 | EN 1991-3 |
| Road vehicle on bridge | 1.25–1.50 | EN 1991-2 |
| Forklift on industrial floor | 1.40 | EN 1991-1 |

The design load is therefore: `F_design = phi * F_static`

For crane beams, additionally check the horizontal surge force (20 % of crane bridge weight applied laterally) and longitudinal braking force (10 % of sum of all wheel loads). These secondary forces frequently govern the connection design.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Should I check bending stress or deflection first?**
Always check deflection (serviceability limit state, SLS) first for beams in buildings — it is almost always the governing criterion. Typical limits: L/250 for floors under imposed loads; L/200 for crane beams; L/400 for brittle finishes. Strength (ULS) may govern for heavily loaded transfer beams or short spans.

**What is the deflection formula for a cantilever under a point load at the free end?**
`delta_max = F * L^3 / (3 * E * I)` at the free tip. For a cantilever under uniform load: `delta_max = q * L^4 / (8 * E * I)`. E = 210,000 MPa for structural steel; I in mm^4; F in N; L in mm → result in mm.

**What is the most material-efficient method for a heavily loaded simply supported beam?**
Use a haunch (tapered section deeper at midspan) or a plate girder with variable flange thickness. Both methods concentrate material where the bending moment is highest. For spans above 12 m in industrial buildings, a welded plate girder with a depth-to-span ratio of L/12 to L/15 is typically the most economical solution.
