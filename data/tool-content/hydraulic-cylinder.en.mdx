## When to Use and When Not to Use

### When to Use

- **Industrial Hydraulic Actuator Sizing:** Calculate the required bore diameter and rod diameter to deliver target push and pull forces for metal forming presses, plastic injection molding clamping units, scissor lift tables, and machine tool clamping fixtures.
- **Cycle Time and Speed Asymmetry Balancing:** Evaluate the kinematic velocity difference between extension (slower, full piston area) and retraction (faster, annular area) in single-rod differential cylinders to program machine sequencing and throughput.
- **Hydraulic Power Unit (HPU) Sizing:** Determine the required pump flow delivery (L/min or gpm) and prime mover shaft power (kW or hp) under full system pressure to properly size electric motors and reservoir cooling capacities.
- **Seal Friction and Mechanical Efficiency Assessment:** Quantify how friction across piston seals, rod gland seals, and wear rings (typically 85 to 95 percent mechanical efficiency) attenuates theoretical output forces.
- **Mobile Equipment Circuit Verification:** Check available thrust and retraction tonnage under working pressure in excavator booms, front-loader bucket cylinders, and outrigger stabilizing legs.

### When Not to Use

- **Long-Stroke Piston Rod Buckling (Column Failure):** When cylinder stroke exceeds 10 times the rod diameter, the rod is susceptible to elastic Euler or inelastic Tetmajer buckling under compressive thrust; Euler column stability must be verified independently.
- **High-Speed Servo-Hydraulic Dynamic Positioning:** At piston velocities exceeding 0.5 m/s, fluid compressibility, proportional valve latency, moving mass inertia, and hydraulic resonance dominate performance; frequency response analysis and state-space simulation are required.
- **Regenerative Differential Circuits:** In regenerative modes where discharge from the rod end is redirected back into the bore end, speed and force relationships differ (thrust depends on rod area alone, while speed scales with the rod-to-bore area ratio).
- **End-of-Stroke Deceleration Cushioning Pressure Spikes:** As the piston enters internal cushioning spears at high velocity, local trapped chamber pressures can peak at 2 to 3 times nominal system pressure, demanding separate tube wall hoop stress checks.
- **Telescopic Multi-Stage Cylinders:** Nested multi-stage telescopic cylinders feature decreasing bore areas and varying stroke limits per stage; each stage must be calculated as an independent cylinder.

## Formula Derivation and Physical Background

Double-acting single-rod hydraulic cylinders convert hydrostatic fluid pressure energy into linear mechanical thrust and displacement through differential piston areas.

### 1. Piston Bore and Annular Area Derivation

During the extension stroke (push mode), pressurized hydraulic oil acts across the entire circular cross-section of the cylinder bore:

<Formula label="Piston Full Bore Area">
A_bore = (pi * D^2) / 4   [m²]
</Formula>

Where D is the internal cylinder bore diameter (in metres).

During the retraction stroke (pull mode), the solid steel piston rod occupies central volume within the rod-end chamber. Consequently, pressurized fluid acts only across the annular ring area:

<Formula label="Rod-End Annulus Area">
A_annulus = A_bore - A_rod = (pi / 4) * (D^2 - d^2)   [m²]
</Formula>

Where d is the piston rod diameter (in metres). The rod diameter must always remain strictly smaller than the bore diameter (d less than D).

### 2. Theoretical and Actual Hydraulic Forces (Pascal's Principle)

According to Pascal's law, hydrostatic pressure p applied to a confined fluid acts equally in all directions normal to containing surfaces. The theoretical static force is:

```
F_theoretical = p * A
```

With pressure p in Pascals (1 bar = 100,000 Pa = 0.1 N/mm²) and area A in square metres, force is obtained in Newtons (or divided by 1000 for kiloNewtons):
- **Theoretical Extend Force:** F_ext = (p · A_bore) / 1000  [kN]
- **Theoretical Retract Force:** F_ret = (p · A_annulus) / 1000  [kN]

Frictional resistance generated by elastomer piston seals, polyurethane rod U-cups, and composite guide strips reduces usable output force. Incorporating mechanical efficiency eta_m (percentage):

<Formula label="Actual Useful Thrust Force">
F_actual = F_theoretical * (eta_m / 100)   [kN]
</Formula>

In standard industrial cylinders, eta_m typically ranges from 85 to 95 percent.

### 3. Extension and Retraction Velocities (Volumetric Continuity)

By conservation of mass for an incompressible fluid, flow rate Q (in m³/s, where 1 L/min = 1 / 60,000 m³/s) entering a chamber drives the piston at a linear velocity proportional to the active chamber area:

<Formula label="Extension Velocity">
v_ext = (Q / A_bore) * 1000   [mm/s]
</Formula>

<Formula label="Retraction Velocity">
v_ret = (Q / A_annulus) * 1000   [mm/s]
</Formula>

Because A_annulus is smaller than A_bore, **retraction is inherently faster than extension** at any constant pump supply flow rate. The area ratio phi = A_bore / A_annulus defines the velocity multiplier.

### 4. Required Hydraulic Input Power

The hydraulic fluid power delivered by the pumping system to overcome circuit pressure at rated flow is the direct product of pressure and volumetric delivery:

<Formula label="Hydraulic Input Power">
P_hydraulic = (p * Q) / 1000 = (p_bar * 100,000 * (Q_Lpm / 60,000)) / 1000 = (p_bar * Q_Lpm) / 600   [kW]
</Formula>

Substituting pressure in bar and flow in L/min, the practical constant 600 yields the exact hydraulic power in kiloWatts.

## Worked Example — Default Calculator Inputs

The following step-by-step calculation validates the tool against its factory-default input parameters:

### Input Values:
- Operating Pressure (p): **160 bar = 16,000,000 Pa (16 MPa)**
- Cylinder Bore Diameter (D): **80 mm = 0.080 m**
- Piston Rod Diameter (d): **45 mm = 0.045 m**
- Pump Supply Flow Rate (Q): **25 L/min = 25 / 60,000 m³/s = 0.00041667 m³/s**
- Mechanical Cylinder Efficiency (eta_m): **90 percent (0.90)**

### Step 1 — Cross-Sectional Areas:

Full bore area (extension chamber):

```
A_bore = (pi * 0.080^2) / 4 = pi * 0.0064 / 4 = 0.00502655 m² = 50.27 cm²
```

Rod cross-sectional area:

```
A_rod = (pi * 0.045^2) / 4 = pi * 0.002025 / 4 = 0.00159043 m² = 15.90 cm²
```

Annulus area (retraction chamber):

```
A_annulus = A_bore - A_rod = 0.00502655 - 0.00159043 = 0.00343612 m² = 34.36 cm²
```

### Step 2 — Theoretical Output Forces:

Under 160 bar (16,000,000 Pa) hydraulic pressure:

```
F_ext_theor = (16,000,000 N/m² * 0.00502655 m²) / 1000 = 80,424.8 N = 80.42 kN
F_ret_theor = (16,000,000 N/m² * 0.00343612 m²) / 1000 = 54,977.9 N = 54.98 kN
```

Rounded to one decimal place on the results display: **80.4 kN** extend force and **55.0 kN** retract force.

### Step 3 — Actual Usable Output Forces (90% Mechanical Efficiency):

Accounting for internal seal and wiper friction:

```
F_ext_act = 80.425 kN * 0.90 = 72.38 kN
F_ret_act = 54.978 kN * 0.90 = 49.48 kN
```

The usable mechanical thrust delivered to the load is **72.4 kN** on extend and **49.5 kN** on retract.

### Step 4 — Linear Actuation Speeds:

With flow rate Q = 25 / 60,000 = 0.00041667 m³/s:

```
v_ext = (0.00041667 m³/s / 0.00502655 m²) * 1000 = 82.89 mm/s
v_ret = (0.00041667 m³/s / 0.00343612 m²) * 1000 = 121.26 mm/s
```

Displaying as **82.9 mm/s** extend speed and **121.3 mm/s** retract speed.

The speed magnification factor is:

```
v_ret / v_ext = 121.26 / 82.89 = 1.463
```

Retraction is 46.3 percent faster than extension due to the 31.6 percent area reduction caused by the 45 mm rod.

### Step 5 — Hydraulic Power Demand:

```
P_hydraulic = (160 bar * 25 L/min) / 600 = 4000 / 600 = 6.67 kW
```

The hydraulic power consumed is **6.67 kW**. When selecting an electric drive motor, factoring in typical pump volumetric and mechanical losses (overall pump efficiency around 85 percent) dictates a motor nameplate rating of at least 8.5 to 11 kW.

## Common Engineering Mistakes

1. **Calculating Retraction Force with Bore Area:** Using the full 80 mm bore area for retraction overestimates pull capacity by 46 percent (80.4 kN instead of 55.0 kN). In vertical lifting mechanisms where the cylinder pulls a load downward against friction, this mistake causes stalled motion.
2. **Ignoring Electric Motor and Pump Efficiency:** The calculated 6.67 kW represents net fluid work. Neglecting pump volumetric efficiency (typically 88 to 92 percent) and electric motor efficiency (88 to 93 percent) results in undersized prime movers that trip thermal overload breakers during sustained cycling.
3. **Overlooking Column Buckling on Extended Strokes:** An 80 mm bore cylinder with a 45 mm rod pushing 72 kN over a stroke greater than 700 to 1000 mm is prone to sudden lateral rod buckling. Pin-to-pin mounting conditions must be checked against Euler or Perry-Robertson column formulas.
4. **Neglecting Return Line Back-Pressure:** Tank return lines, directional valves, and return filter elements create 5 to 15 bar back-pressure in the opposing chamber. In differential cylinders, this opposing pressure acts against the larger bore area during retraction, diminishing net pull force.
5. **Under-Sizing Tank Return Piping for Annular Displacement:** During rapid cylinder extension, the volume entering the bore end is 25 L/min, but during rapid retraction, fluid exiting the bore end surges to 25 · (50.27 / 34.36) = 36.6 L/min. Sizing return filters solely for pump flow causes pressure spikes and reservoir aeration.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### 1. Why does a single-rod hydraulic cylinder retract faster than it extends?

In a single-rod differential cylinder, the rod occupies part of the internal volume on the rod-end side. For a constant pump delivery of 25 L/min, the smaller annular volume fills more rapidly than the larger full-bore volume, resulting in a higher linear velocity on retraction (121.3 mm/s vs 82.9 mm/s). However, because pressure acts on a smaller net area, retraction force is proportionally lower.

### 2. How are standard industrial bore and rod diameter combinations selected?

ISO 3320 and ISO 6020-2 define standardized nominal bore-to-rod diameter pairings based on area ratio phi:
- phi = 1.43 (such as 80 mm bore with 45 mm rod), providing a balanced compromise between push and pull force for general automation.
- phi = 2.00 (such as 80 mm bore with 56 mm rod), offering heavier rod column buckling resistance for harsh construction and earthmoving equipment.
- phi = 1.25 (such as 80 mm bore with 36 mm rod), maximizing pull force where buckling risk is minimal.

### 3. What factors influence cylinder mechanical efficiency in practice?

Mechanical efficiency reflects frictional resistance at the elastomeric piston seals, rod gland U-cups, scraper rings, and bronze-filled PTFE wear rings. Under low operating pressures (20 to 50 bar), seal pre-compression accounts for a larger proportion of total force, reducing efficiency to 80 to 85 percent. At rated pressures between 160 and 250 bar, frictional losses are small relative to hydraulic thrust, raising efficiency to 90 to 95 percent.

### 4. How can heat buildup in continuous-duty cylinders be mitigated?

Frictional drag across seals and pressure drops across throttling valves convert hydraulic energy into heat. If hydraulic oil temperature exceeds 60°C, seal elastomers harden and accelerate internal leakage. Hydraulic reservoir volume should equal 3 to 5 times pump flow per minute, and an air-blast or water-cooled heat exchanger should be incorporated for duty cycles exceeding 50 percent.

### 5. What is a regenerative circuit and when should it be used?

A regenerative circuit connects both the bore end and rod end to system pressure simultaneously during extension. Fluid displaced from the rod end is recirculated directly into the bore end alongside pump flow, boosting extension speed by the factor A_bore / A_rod. However, net forward thrust is reduced to pressure multiplied by the rod area (p · A_rod). It is commonly used for rapid advance strokes in machine presses before high-pressure pressing begins.
